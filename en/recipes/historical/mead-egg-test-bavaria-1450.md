## Egg-Test Mead with Beer Barm (Bavaria, mid-15th c.)

(translated by u/ralfv)

*New to these? Read the [introduction](/recipes/historical) first: it explains what is left out on purpose and what the yeast of the time could do.*

**Source:** Two nearly identical Bavarian manuscripts of the mid-15th century: Berlin, Staatsbibliothek, Ms. germ. qu. 1187, recipe 267 (complete), and Vienna, Austrian National Library, Cod. 2897, recipe 269 (breaks off after the skimming).

**Language:** Early New High German (Bavarian)

**Style:** Traditional

### Translation

*How you shall make mead.*

Take honey, as much as you want to have, as much of it as is in the comb. Then take twice as much water, pour it on, boil (?) it out clean. Take a fresh egg and lay it in; add water for as long as it takes until the egg shows. If you cannot get an egg, take a fresh *Regelbirne* [a pear]: so one sees when it has enough.

Let it run through a cloth so that the wax comes out of it. Put it in a kettle, boil it clean and skim it clean, and take a little bag, put the yeast (?) in it, not too much of it, and let it boil. When it has boiled well, right down to the mark, take a clean tub, pour it in, and let it get cold, so that you can well bear your hand in it and it is not too hot. And put beer barm into it and let it stand. Then it becomes good and clear of the barm.

### Notes

- **Egg test:** water is added until the egg "shows" above the surface: the old way of setting the honey strength. A fresh pear (*Regelspiern*) can stand in for the egg.
- ***Regelbirne*:** a named old pear variety; *-spiern* is the Bavarian spelling of *Birne*, pear. Other cookbooks of the time write it *Regelpieren*, *regelspirren* or *regelbiren*, and old dictionaries equate it with the "king pear". The variety survives in Switzerland as Chriesibirne, a small, long-stalked cooking and drying pear. For the test, any small, firm pear will do.
- ***in dem werch*** ("in the work"): read as the comb. The honey is taken with its comb, which is why the wax is strained out later.
- ***pall es rain aus*:** read as "boil it out clean"; "wash it out clean" is also possible.
- ***heffen* in the little bag:** usually read as yeast; elsewhere in these manuscripts the same word means "pot". The pitching yeast proper is the beer barm added after cooling. If it is yeast, boiling a measured amount of it in a bag in the must is exactly the modern trick of [boiled bread yeast as a Fermaid O substitute](/ingredients/nutrients#using-boiled-bread-yeast): dead yeast feeding the live yeast. Nobody in 1450 knew about nitrogen, but "not too much of it" sounds like someone who had learned what works.
